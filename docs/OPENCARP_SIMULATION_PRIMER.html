From Equations to Rotors: What openCARP Actually Simulates

The bidomain / monodomain tutorial derived the partial differential equation that describes electrical propagation in heart tissue. This document is about the layer on top of it: what a solver like openCARP takes as input, what the ionic model hidden inside \(I_\text{ion}\) actually is, and — the part that matters most for this project — how a self-sustaining spiral wave, a rotor, emerges from those equations, and why the point at its centre is the thing Phase 2 labels as an ablation target.

Why this document exists: Phases 2–4 of this project rest entirely on openCARP simulations. Defending that work in a conversation means being able to say what the solver does, what the membrane model is, why a rotor forms, what its core means, and what the 2‑D monodomain setup leaves out. It does not mean re-deriving the finite-element discretization or the Courtemanche gating kinetics from memory. This primer targets the first list, not the second.

01 · What openCARP solves

A reaction–diffusion equation, marched forward on a mesh

You should come out of this section able to state, without notation, the two things happening at every point in the tissue every time step, and what openCARP needs from you to run.

The tutorial's monodomain equation, written once more so this document stands on its own:

monodomain \[ \chi\!\left( C_m \frac{\partial \Vm}{\partial t} + I_\text{ion}(\Vm, \mathbf{w}) \right) \;=\; \nabla\!\cdot\!\big( \boldsymbol{\sigma}_m \nabla \Vm \big) \;+\; I_\text{stim} \]

Read it as two halves joined by an equals sign, because that is exactly how the solver treats it. The right-hand side is diffusion: the \(\nabla\!\cdot\!(\sigma_m \nabla \Vm)\) term spreads transmembrane voltage from a depolarized patch into its not-yet-depolarized neighbours, the way heat spreads down a metal bar. On its own, diffusion only ever smooths things out — it could never sustain a travelling wave. The left-hand side is the reaction: \(I_\text{ion}\) is the net current through the cell membrane, and it is regenerative — when a patch of membrane is pushed just past a threshold, its ion channels open and actively drive the voltage the rest of the way up, dumping in far more charge than diffusion delivered. That regeneration is what lets a wavefront travel across a whole atrium without dying out: each strip of tissue relights the next.

What “a simulation” is here

openCARP discretizes the tissue into a mesh of nodes. At every node it stores the transmembrane voltage \(\Vm\) and the internal state \(\mathbf{w}\) of the ionic model (gate positions, ion concentrations — §2). Then it marches time forward in small steps. Each step, at every node, it does two things:

  1. The diffusion update — a finite-element solve over the whole mesh that moves \(\Vm\) between neighbouring nodes according to the local conductivity.
  2. The reaction update — an independent ordinary-differential-equation solve at each node separately, advancing that node's ionic model by one step given its current \(\Vm\). This is embarrassingly parallel and is where most of the compute goes.

(Splitting the step into “diffuse, then react” rather than solving the coupled system all at once is called operator splitting; it is standard and it is why the ionic model can be swapped out without touching the diffusion solver.)

What you provide

A mesh (§3); a conductivity field (§3); an ionic model by name (§2); a stimulus protocol — where and when to inject current (§4); and a duration. openCARP returns \(\Vm(\mathbf{x}, t)\) sampled on the mesh — and, if you ask, activation-time maps, state checkpoints, and more. In this project the Python package carputils assembles the command line and manages the run; openCARP itself is a compiled C++ solver.

02 · The ionic model

What is actually inside \(I_\text{ion}\)

You should come out able to explain what a gating variable is, why the cardiac action potential has a plateau, and what “Courtemanche with AF remodeling” means and why this project uses it.

The tutorial treated \(I_\text{ion}(\Vm, \mathbf{w})\) as a black box: “the net membrane current, some function of voltage and internal state.” Opening the box is the difference between saying “a wave propagates” and being able to say why the tissue stays unexcitable for 250 ms afterwards — which is the fact that makes reentry possible.

Channels and gates

The membrane is a capacitor (it holds charge, \(C_m\)) with a set of ion channels wired across it in parallel. Each channel type carries one ion species — sodium, potassium, calcium — and its current is a conductance times a driving force:

\[ I_\text{X} \;=\; g_\text{X}\cdot p_\text{X}(\Vm, t)\cdot\big(\Vm - E_\text{X}\big) \]

\(E_\text{X}\) is the reversal potential for that ion (set by its concentration ratio across the membrane); \(g_\text{X}\) is the maximum conductance; and \(p_\text{X}\) — the important part — is the fraction of that channel population currently open. That fraction is not fixed. It is controlled by gating variables: numbers between 0 and 1 that chase a voltage-dependent target with a voltage-dependent time constant,

\[ \frac{dg}{dt} \;=\; \frac{g_\infty(\Vm) - g}{\tau(\Vm)} \]

each one its own little ODE. This is the Hodgkin–Huxley idea, transplanted from squid axon to cardiac myocyte. A sodium channel, for instance, has a fast activation gate that snaps open when the voltage rises and a slower inactivation gate that then swings shut — so the channel conducts only during a brief window, produces the upstroke, and then stays inactivated until the voltage falls back down and resets it. That last clause is the refractory period, and it is entirely a property of gating kinetics.

The plateau is the whole reason reentry is possible

A squid axon spike is over in ~2 ms. A cardiac action potential lasts 200–300 ms, almost all of it plateau. During that time the tissue is refractory — it has just fired and cannot fire again. Multiply that refractory duration by the conduction velocity and you get the wavelength: the physical length of tissue that is “used up” behind a wavefront at any instant. Reentry — a wave chasing its own tail around a circuit — can only happen if the circuit is longer than the wavelength, so that by the time the front comes back around, the tissue has recovered. Everything in §4 and §5 is about engineering that condition.

Courtemanche, and “AF remodeling”

This project uses the Courtemanche–Ramirez–Nattel (1998) model: a human atrial myocyte, roughly 21 state variables (the voltage plus ~20 gates and internal ion concentrations). Atrial, because the target arrhythmia — atrial fibrillation — is an atrial disease, and atrial cells repolarize differently from ventricular ones.

Persistent AF physically remodels the atrial myocyte: channel expression shifts over weeks. The standard in-silico stand-in, which the openCARP example applies, is to rescale a handful of maximal conductances — reduce the L-type calcium current \(g_\text{CaL}\) by about half, increase the inward-rectifier \(g_\text{K1}\), cut the transient outward current, and so on. The net effect of all of it is what matters:

  • a shorter action potential — less plateau, faster repolarization;
  • a flatter restitution curve — the action potential barely shortens further as the heart rate rises, which removes a natural brake on wavebreak;
  • together with the deliberately slowed conduction (§3), a much shorter wavelength.

A shorter wavelength means a reentrant circuit fits in a smaller piece of tissue — so a rotor can be induced on a 5 cm sheet and, once induced, stays stable rather than self-terminating. Without the remodeling, this substrate would not reliably hold a rotor. The remodeled model is not a detail; it is what makes the whole Phase 2 pipeline work.

03 · Mesh and substrate

The sheet of tissue and the scar in the middle of it

You should come out able to describe the geometry, say how conductivity relates to conduction velocity, and explain what the central fibrotic patch is and why it is there.

The mesh

A 5 cm × 5 cm square sheet, triangulated with an average edge length of 0.4 mm — a regular 126×126 grid, so 15,876 nodes. Every node carries a transmembrane voltage and a full copy of the ionic model's state. Every triangular element carries a conductivity tensor and an integer region tag that says which tissue type it is (healthy, or inside the patch).

It is a sheet, not a slab — two-dimensional, one notional cell layer thick. There is no wall thickness, therefore no through-wall (“transmural”) direction, no fibre rotation through the wall, and no scroll waves. That is a real simplification and §8 lists what it costs.

Conductivity sets the conduction velocity

The speed a planar wavefront travels scales, roughly, as the square root of the conductivity: \(\text{CV} \propto \sqrt{\sigma / (\chi C_m)}\). You do not usually set \(\sigma\) directly to a physical number and hope; instead you pick the conduction velocity you want and let a helper (openCARP's tuneCV) search for the \(\sigma\) that produces it on this mesh at this resolution. This example tunes to 0.3 m/s — deliberately slow. Healthy atrial conduction is more like 0.6–1.0 m/s; the slowed value is part of the same “shorten the wavelength so a rotor fits” strategy as the AF remodeling.

One anisotropy caveat

The tutorial spent real effort on anisotropy — conduction being faster along muscle fibres than across them. This particular 2‑D mesh is isotropic: the longitudinal and transverse conductivities are set equal (\(g_{il} = g_{it}\)), so there is no fibre direction at all. Everything in the fibre-orientation discussion elsewhere in this project's notes is about a effect that is deliberately absent from this setup, not one that is modelled in it.

The fibrotic patch — the reentry substrate

At the centre of the sheet is a disc of radius ~1.4 cm of “fibrotic” tissue. Inside that disc:

  • 30% of the elements, chosen at random, are made nearly non-conductive (conductivity set to \(10^{-7}\) S/m — effectively tiny holes the wave must go around);
  • the other 70% get the AF ionic remodeling — rescaled channel conductances representing the effect of inflammatory signalling on surviving myocytes.

The point of the patch is to create a region where conduction is slow, uneven, and fragile — where a wavefront threading between the holes can be delayed, split, or blocked on one side but not the other. That is precisely the condition a rotor needs to be born (§5), and once one exists, the patch edge gives its core somewhere stable to anchor. Real atrial fibrosis is patchy and diffuse in a way this single clean disc only cartoons — but the mechanism it provides is the right one.

04 · Stimulation and induction

How you start a wave, and how you turn a wave into a rotor

You should come out able to explain what a stimulus is, why the tissue is “prepaced” first, and what the rapid-pacing protocol this project uses is doing.

A stimulus is an injected current applied to a chosen set of nodes for a few milliseconds — long enough to push their \(\Vm\) past the sodium-channel threshold and launch a self-propagating wave. That is the only way anything happens in the simulation; with no stimulus the tissue just sits at rest.

Prepacing

Before any arrhythmia induction, the tissue is prepaced: stimulated repeatedly at a fixed, comfortable rate (basic cycle length 500 ms, a few beats) until every node has settled onto a stable, repeating limit cycle — ion concentrations, gate positions, everything periodic. The final state is saved as a checkpoint. Induction protocols start from that checkpoint, so that what they do is a clean perturbation of a steady state rather than something tangled up with start-up transients.

A real gotcha from this project

The openCARP Docker image ships a pre-computed prepace checkpoint from 2020, in an older on-disk format. run.py silently reuses it, and restoring that stale state into the current solver made runs fail with no error message. The fix (in opencarp/README.md) was to delete it once so it regenerates against the current binary. Worth knowing as an example of how much of simulation work is plumbing.

Induction protocols

All of these are trying to produce one thing: a unidirectional conduction block — a spot where a wave can pass in one direction but not the other — because that is the seed of a rotor (§5).

S1–S2 cross-field
Launch a planar wave (S1) from one edge. Then, timed to land in the repolarizing tail of S1, fire a second stimulus (S2) from a perpendicular edge. The part of S2 that lands on still-refractory tissue is blocked; the part on recovered tissue propagates — and now has a free end that curls. The textbook method.
Rapid pacing — RP_B
What this project uses. Pace repeatedly from one site with a progressively shorter interval between beats (200 ms down to 100 ms in 10 ms steps). As the interval closes in on the tissue's refractory period, some beat catches part of the tissue not yet recovered → local block → wavebreak → reentry. The _B variant checks for reentry after every beat. For rotor A, the beat at basic cycle length 150 ms, from node 7830, was the one that took.
PSD — phase-singularity distribution
Skip the physics of induction. Place a phase singularity by hand at a chosen point, solve the Eikonal (wavefront-arrival-time) equation for an activation map consistent with a rotor there, and start the monodomain simulation from that. Reliable and fast; less physiological, since you assumed the rotor rather than triggering it.
PEERP
Pace timed to the local effective refractory period to drop a single well-placed ectopic beat. A more surgical version of rapid pacing.

05 · How a rotor forms

From a straight wavefront to a wave that chases its own tail forever

This is the section to actually understand. You should come out able to narrate, in four steps, how a premature beat on the right substrate becomes a self-sustaining spiral, and what its “core” is.

Three ingredients: a wavefront, the refractory tail that follows it, and a trigger that makes a new front run into that tail.

Why it sustains — wavelength versus circuit length

The rotor keeps going for exactly one reason: everywhere the rotating front arrives, the tissue has just barely finished recovering from the previous rotation. The wavelength — conduction velocity × refractory period — is shorter than the path the front travels per rotation. If the wavelength were longer, the front would catch its own refractory tail, hit block, and the rotor would extinguish. This is why the AF remodeling (shorter refractory period, §2) and the slowed conduction (§3) are both needed: each shortens the wavelength, and together they open a window in which a rotor on a 5 cm sheet is not just inducible but stable.

The core, and anchoring

The core is the small region the spiral tip circles. Tissue there is perpetually caught between excitation and recovery — never fully depolarized, never fully at rest. That is why, in this project's 5 s recordings, the transmembrane-voltage amplitude is measurably lower near the core and rises with distance from it: the core is a hole of disorganized, low-amplitude activity at the centre of an otherwise crisply periodic field.

A free-floating rotor core also tends to drift, and it often drifts until it catches on a conduction heterogeneity — here, the edge of the fibrotic patch — and then sits there. This is why, in the regenerated runs, each rotor's core wanders for the first second or two and then settles onto a stable anchor. It is also why the substrate (§3) is not just decoration: it determines where the rotor ends up, which is exactly what an ablation-targeting model needs to predict.

The link to the rest of the project

This rotor is the simulation's model of an atrial-fibrillation driver. Clinically, one ablation strategy is to destroy the rotor core so the arrhythmia can no longer organize around it. Phase 2 labels the core region as the ablation target; Phase 3 will actually test, in silico, whether ablating there (or elsewhere) terminates the rotor.

06 · Phase singularities

Turning “where is the rotor core” into a number the computer can find

You should come out able to explain what phase means here, what a phase singularity is, and why the singularity's location is taken as the ablation target.

Once a rotor is running, every point in the tissue is doing the same cycle — depolarize, plateau, repolarize, rest — just out of step with its neighbours. That “where in the cycle are you right now” is a natural thing to turn into an angle.

Assigning a phase

Take one node's \(\Vm(t)\) trace over the recording. Subtract its mean so it oscillates around zero. Apply the Hilbert transform — the same tool the tutorial mentioned for turning a real oscillation into a rotating complex number — to get an analytic signal \(V(t) + i\,\hat V(t)\). Its argument is the instantaneous phase \(\theta(t) \in (-\pi, \pi]\): \(-\pi\) at deep rest, climbing through 0 at the upstroke, wrapping back around each cycle. Do this independently at all 15,876 nodes and the “movie of voltage” becomes a “movie of phase.”

Finding the singularity

This is what opencarp/phase_singularity.py does, and it is pure topology: for every small cell of the mesh grid, add up the wrapped phase differences going around its four corners. For an ordinary patch of tissue that sum is ~0. Around a singularity it is \(\pm 2\pi\) — you cannot continuously assign phase there. Mark those cells each frame, link the one core across frames into a trajectory by nearest-neighbour continuity, and you have the rotor core's path.

Why this is the ablation target

Nodes within a fixed radius (3 mm) of the singularity's trajectory are labelled the ablation-target ground truth; everything else is background. This is not an arbitrary heuristic — phase-singularity tracking is the standard way rotor cores are identified in cardiac optical-mapping experiments, and ablating the core is a real (if clinically debated) strategy for terminating atrial fibrillation. Phase 2's entire ML task is: predict that label from local electrical features, without having to compute the phase field.

07 · Running the pipeline

The actual sequence of steps, and what each one writes to disk

You should come out able to walk through the run end to end and name the key numerical settings and output files.

time step
\(dt = 20\,\mu\text{s}\). Small because the sodium upstroke is fast and the ionic ODEs are stiff; too large a step and the solution blows up or the upstroke is smeared.
output interval
spacedt = 1 ms — \(\Vm\) is written for all nodes every millisecond, not every solver step. A 5 s run is 5001 frames × 15,876 nodes ≈ 300 MB.
mass lumping
On. Approximates the finite-element mass matrix as diagonal, making each diffusion solve much cheaper; accurate enough on a regular mesh like this one.
vm.igb
openCARP's binary “field over time” format. carputils has a Python reader; this project re-exports to a portable .npz so the notebooks need no Docker.
state.*.roe
A full checkpoint of every node's complete ionic state at one instant. It lets you restart the simulation from exactly there — which is the mechanism Phase 3 uses to branch off dozens of “what if we ablate here” counterfactuals from a single established rotor.

The free-running window was originally ~400 ms — about two rotor rotations, which turned out to be too short for any frequency- or rate-based feature to mean anything. It was re-run at ~5 s (about 24 rotations). On this 2‑D mesh, 8 CPU cores, that free-run takes roughly three minutes of wall-clock. That number is the baseline Phase 4's surrogate has to beat, and Phase 3's ablation sweep multiplies it by the number of candidate sites — which is the whole reason a fast surrogate is worth building.

08 · What it does and doesn't capture

The simplifications, each named once

You should come out able to volunteer these before someone asks — naming your own model's limits reads as competence, not weakness.

ChoiceWhat it costs
Monodomain, not bidomainOne combined conductivity, no separate extracellular potential. No true unipolar/bipolar electrogram, no virtual-electrode or defibrillation physics. Phase 2 approximates the electrogram as local \(\Vm\); PHASE2_METHODOLOGY.md §7 owns this.
2‑D sheetNo wall thickness → no transmural fibre rotation, no scroll waves, no endocardial/epicardial dissociation.
Isotropic conduction\(g_{il} = g_{it}\): no fibre direction at all. Real atrial conduction is 2–3× faster along fibres; that anisotropy is simply absent here.
Idealized substrateA single circular fibrotic disc with random 30% dropout — a cartoon of real, diffuse, patchy atrial fibrosis.
Courtemanche + a fixed AF rescaleOne of several human atrial ionic models; the remodeling multipliers are representative literature values, not patient-specific.
One rotor per runReal AF often shows multiple, meandering, transient rotors and fibrillatory conduction. This is the stable-single-rotor idealization.
No mechano-electric feedback, autonomic tone, or metabolic stateThe tissue does not contract, and nothing modulates it beat to beat.
Lesion = instantaneous conductivity change (Phase 3)The electroporation pulse itself — its field, its dose, any acute pro-arrhythmic effect — is not modelled. That is deferred to the optional Phase 5.

None of these invalidate the project. They set its scope: a controlled, inspectable, fast-to-iterate model in which “where is the rotor, and what happens if we ablate here” is a well-posed question with a checkable answer — which is exactly what a surrogate needs to be trained and validated against.

09 · Check yourself

Four questions, no math

If you can answer these out loud, you can defend the simulation layer of this project.

warm-upWhy doesn't diffusion alone propagate a wave?

Answer in terms of what each half of the reaction–diffusion equation contributes.

Show answer

Diffusion only ever moves existing voltage around and smooths gradients out — it is dissipative, so a bump of depolarization would spread thinner and fade. What keeps a wavefront alive is the reaction term: when diffusion nudges a fresh patch of membrane past threshold, that patch's own ion channels open and actively inject far more charge than diffusion delivered, regenerating a full-amplitude action potential. Each strip of tissue relights the next. Remove the ionic model and you have heat flow, not a wave.

coreWhy do both AF remodeling and slowed conduction matter for getting a stable rotor?

Use the idea of wavelength.

Show answer

Wavelength = conduction velocity × refractory period: the length of tissue “used up” behind a wavefront. A reentrant circuit sustains only if it is longer than the wavelength, so the returning front always meets recovered tissue. AF remodeling shortens the refractory period (shorter action potential); the deliberate CV tuning to 0.3 m/s shortens the velocity. Both shrink the wavelength, and together they make it small enough that a rotor fits on a 5 cm sheet and, once formed, does not catch its own tail and die.

coreWhat is the rotor core, physically, and why is voltage amplitude lower there?
Show answer

The core is the small region the spiral tip pivots around but never sweeps through cleanly. Tissue there is held perpetually between excitation and recovery — it never fully depolarizes and never fully rests — so its transmembrane-voltage swing is small and disorganized compared with tissue a few millimetres away that gets a clean full action potential once per rotation. That is why, in the 5 s recordings, peak-to-peak amplitude rises monotonically with distance from the core (correlation ~0.45–0.5 across the three rotors) — and why amplitude, not dominant frequency, is the feature that tracks core proximity.

stretchA colleague says “your electrogram features are meaningless because it's only monodomain.” What's the honest response?
Show answer

They have a real point and it should be conceded plainly: monodomain has no separate extracellular potential, so a true unipolar or bipolar electrogram — which is a spatial derivative of extracellular potential — cannot be computed from it. This project approximates the electrogram as local \(\Vm\) (and simple differences of it), which is a documented shortcut, not a derivation. What that costs: absolute amplitudes and exact waveform morphology are not trustworthy. What survives: the relative spatial structure — that activity is lower-amplitude and more disorganized near the core than far from it — is a property of \(\Vm\) itself and does carry over. The right framing is that the model supports questions about spatial organization around a driver, not questions about electrogram morphology, and the feature work stays on the right side of that line. A full answer would move to bidomain with an extracellular solve — which is the 3‑D non-contact direction this project scoped and deferred.
